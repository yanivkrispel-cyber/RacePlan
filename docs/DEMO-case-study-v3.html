Brief

For people quietly failing at home plants.

Every stage runs end to end: client definition, competitor review, in-depth interviews, persona, feature strategy, screen design and a working product. The method is deliberately sharp — two in-depth interviews instead of a broad survey, one focused persona, and a narrow MVP that proves the core value before anything else is built.

The audience: home growers, from one windowsill plant to dozens across rooms and offices. They love it, but care depends on many variables — watering, light, method, conditions — and they lose track. The task: centralize the plant list, explain how to grow each one, remind me to water, and leave room for a social layer.

“I have loads of plants at home and many of them wilt on me very quickly. I can't keep track of which ones I watered and which I didn't, how much I watered, how I'm supposed to grow them…” Central quote from the problem definition

Research findings

What existing apps get wrong, and where the opening is.

Directions explored

  • Photograph a plant to add it, or diagnose its health.
  • Watering reminders and growing tips (light, pot, fertilizer, pests).
  • Weather-aware care; camera-based light measurement.
  • Growth photo diary, sharing, cutting swaps, plant-sitters.

Market gaps

  • Pricing trust: Planta locks even reminders behind a paywall — a strong free core forms the habit first.
  • Context UX: the home grower needs status at a glance, no visual noise.
  • Real social: PictureThis feeds are global and impersonal; focused, local sharing is the differentiator.

Differentiators defined

  • My home mode: reminders adapted to the room and apartment, not just the city.
  • Family tree: trace which parent plant a neighbour's cutting came from.
  • Happiness index: one measure of whole-home plant health.
  • Privacy first: background blur, nickname profiles, dosed notifications.

Shared

Both carry plant-loss trauma, both are plant-app virgins needing zero learning curve, and both reject a public feed. The app must be failure-proof and private.

Split

Authority is the fault line: one wants the physically close community that shares her light and A/C; the other wants a certified professional and nothing else.

Implications

A hybrid help model — AI as the in-home expert plus an optional close circle; multi-location management; and preventive alerts before a plant is lost.

Failure-proof

Never an empty, guilt-inducing screen — always what needs care today and what is thriving.

Zero learning curve

One home screen, no tutorial, no nested menus, no page scrolling.

Private by default

Every plant, photo and scan belongs to one account. Nothing is shared without an explicit action.

Design system

Deep foliage glass with a gold accent.

Dark green over the bright white of competing apps: the persona checks her plants in the evening, and the interface should feel like the calm the garden gives her, not a task manager. Gold is reserved strictly for the primary action in each context, so there is never a question about what to press. The health scan uses a stethoscope, not a camera — “examine my plant”, not “take a picture”. Settings sit top-left and notifications top-right on every screen.

Health scale: green ≥ 85% thriving · amber 70–84% needs attention · red < 70% needs you today — the same scale on cards, percentages, graphs and stats.

Screens

Design → working product.

Dashboard

Answers one question — what needs me today. Fits a single phone view with no page scrolling; only the checklist scrolls internally, and the bottom tiles became read-only statuses rather than buttons. Live clock and simulated weather follow the garden location, and the graph plots care tasks actually completed.

My Garden

Room filters come straight from the research: plants in different rooms behave differently, so the room is a first-class attribute. Each card carries a health dot, percentage, age and a water badge.

Plant Detail

The care guide replaces the encyclopedia the persona has no time to read — watering interval, light, temperature and humidity beside a 7-day health chart and one-tap water and fertilize actions.

Add Plant

“Photograph the plant to add it to my list” was the very first thinking direction. Two distinct controls were needed — shoot now, or pick an existing photo — plus room chips and a live type preview.

Health Scanner

The hybrid help model in practice: the AI plays the in-home expert interviewee 2 demanded, returning a grade, confidence, findings and treatment steps from a real photo diagnosis.

Settings, notifications and sign-in

Settings live behind the top-left control on every screen, and the garden location entered there drives the weather and clock. Notifications are preventive — they flag a health drop before it becomes a loss. Sign-up had to be fast: email, Google and one-tap biometrics on one card.

Changes made after seeing it running

  • One-screen dashboard — scrolling defeats status-at-a-glance.
  • Honest graph — plots care tasks completed, not an abstract score.
  • Missed-tasks drawer — overdue items stay tickable instead of vanishing.
  • Statuses, not buttons — the three tiles no longer look tappable.
  • Two photo controls — take photo and upload photo, split apart.
  • Diagnosis moved out of add-plant — adding is collecting; scanning is its own flow.

What comes next

  • More interviews — two respondents already split on authority; the hybrid model needs validation at scale.
  • Usability testing on device with the persona's own plants.
  • The private close-circle layer with blur and nickname profiles.
  • “My home mode” per-room micro-climate and a live weather feed.
  • Growth diary and auto growth video — the emotional pay-off both interviewees hinted at.
